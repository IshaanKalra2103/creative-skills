# The aesthetic, and how to make it your own

This skill was learned from one reel (`case-study.md`), but **the reel is a style reference, not a template.** A new
film shares its look and its rhythm, and nothing else: not its order of shots, not its props, not its sentences.

## What makes the look (keep all of this)

**1. An archive under glass.** Everything sits under one texture: scanlines, a 30° halftone screen, low grain, a
colour fringe on type (`look.md` has the numbers). Photos are real and archival, a little soft, and graded warm or
cool. The flat fields are honest colours that look printed: near-black, grey card, beige paper, a deep red or blue.
Nothing glossy, no gradients, no 3D UI chrome.

**2. Small serif type, big pictures.** Captions are small Instrument Serif, set under or beside the subject: names,
one word, one short question. Type is never the hero, except on a flat card, and even there it stays modest (40-60 px
on a 720-wide frame). Handwriting (Homemade Apple) is for asides and marks; Roboto for anything on a screen.

**3. Motion on threes.** The picture changes 10 times a second. Entries snap in on one tick and settle with a fast
deceleration over 3-7 ticks. A slow push or drift keeps every hold alive, and holds stay at or under ~0.5 s. Blur only
marks speed: a one-tick zoom or whip blur on entry.

**4. One literal picture per spoken phrase.** The edit cuts on content words (0.4-2 s per shot, about 1 s on average).
Each phrase gets the most literal, slightly witty picture for it: "finding" puts a word under a magnifier, an "idea"
gets a hypnotised head, "millions" becomes a tunnel of thumbnails. The joke lives in how literal the picture is.

**5. Three registers, alternating.** Archival photo, then a flat card with type, then a graphic or infographic (a chart,
a globe, a search bar, a counter). Never three of the same register in a row.

**6. Period props, used sparingly.** Old computer UI, printouts, paper and stamps date the story. Pick the props of
**your topic's** era: a 1969 film gets printouts and a DSKY, not a Windows XP window.

## What must change every film (the originality rules)

1. **Your own arc.** Don't open "One X did Y" with a dot-note, then a date card, then the people. Pick an arc that suits
   the topic (below) and write the beat sheet before choosing kinds.
2. **Your own metaphors.** For each beat, ask what the most literal picture of this phrase is, for this topic. List 2-3
   options and pick the one nobody would expect. Reuse a kind only when it really is the best picture.
3. **No signature moves from the reference unless earned.** The reel's signature moves:
   - the dot-grid note opener;
   - the XP window with a globe;
   - "The Internet" zoom-word;
   - the magnifier on a word;
   - the Google search bar;
   - the hypno head;
   - the face cards on red;
   - the green globe grid;
   - the Ae file icon;
   - the blurred website CTA.

   A film may use at most two of them, and only where its own story calls for one.
4. **Compose new shots.** The kinds take params, not fixed pictures: a `photo` takes any plate, camera move and
   caption, a `card` any words on any field, a `chart` any data. When no kind gives the picture a beat needs, add one
   (`spec.md` → Adding a kind). Don't bend the beat to fit a kind that already exists.
5. **Check before rendering.** Write your beat sheet next to the reference's (case-study.md). If three or more
   consecutive beats use the same kinds in the same order, rework them.

## Arcs that suit 20-40 s documentary shorts

| arc | shape | good for |
|---|---|---|
| **In medias res** | the crisis moment → rewind to who and why → how it was solved → the landing → a coda that recontextualises | rescues, failures, close calls (Apollo 1202) |
| **The object** | a thing in close-up → zoom out to where it lived → the people around it → what it changed → it today | inventions, artefacts (the barcode, the floppy) |
| **Number first** | a startling number → what it measures → why it got that big or small → the person or decision behind it → the number again, now understood | scale stories (Voyager's 24 billion km) |
| **Myth vs fact** | the story everyone tells → "but" → the document that shows otherwise → the real story → why the myth stuck | history corrections |
| **Two rivals** | A and B side by side → the race → the turning point → the winner and the cost → where they are now | competitions, format wars |
| **One day** | a clock starts → hour-by-hour beats on a timeline → the moment it turned → the day's legacy | events, disasters, launches |

Hook lines: start inside the moment ("Twelve oh two."), with a number ("Twenty-four billion kilometres."), or with a
contradiction ("The barcode was invented on a beach."). Avoid the reel's "One simple idea changed X forever" template.

## Endings

- **A coda line** that reframes the opening: the stronger default.
- **A call to action** (a URL typed in a search pill on a blurred site) only for a real CTA, such as sharing your
  project or a source. It came from the reel's ad and doesn't belong at the end of every film.
- **A last literal joke**: the final picture pays off a phrase ("burn, baby, burn" in the real source code, highlighted).
