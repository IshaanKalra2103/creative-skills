## Text insights

### Visual philosophy and comic-book language
- **Break up 3D forms with drawn line work so viewers can't tell what is 3D.** Line work is part of the animation, not just the design. Said by Josh Beveridge (Animation Supervisor), PDF 130: *"We have included a lot of line work in the characters' performances. We wanted to break up forms in a way that audiences can't quite tell whether something is 3D or not."*
- **Every character follows the rules of their own universe and medium, even when sharing a frame.** An anime character, a Looney Tunes / *Rocky and Bullwinkle*-style pig and a black-and-white character appear side by side, each rendered in their own style. Phil Lord (producer), PDF 130: *"We have an anime character interacting with Spider-Ham, who is something out of a Rocky and Bullwinkle cartoon, next to a black-and-white character. You think to yourself, 'Wow, it feels like nobody is supervising us. We're not supposed to do this.'"*
- **Stark contrast between characters is a deliberate design value.** Peni stays "anime through and through" next to Miles to create variety. Yashar Kassai (artist), PDF 134: *"One of design's greatest pleasures is stark contrast."*
- **Noir is rendered in gritty black-and-white with a lot of cross-hatching,** because that is the world he comes from. Justin K. Thompson (Production Designer), PDF 140: *"We depict him in gritty black-and-white, because that's the world he is coming from. There's a lot of cross-hatching … involved with Noir."* (The book glosses cross-hatching as "marking or shading two or more intersecting series of parallel lines.")
- **Noir's textures should read as old comic-book or newsprint ink.** His painting was one of the earliest made for the production. Wendell Dalit (development artist), PDF 140: *"Our original idea was to have these textures that feel like an old comic book or newsprint ink."*
- **Reduction is the rule for sets:** strip the world to the fewest shapes that still read. Patrick O'Keefe (artist), PDF 118: *"As with most sets in the film, it was always about seeing how far we could reduce the world around us and how much we could get away with."*
- **Spider-Ham's cartoon physics are allowed.** He throws a black circle on a wall and climbs through it (a portable-hole gag). Loose, graphic, rubber-hose visuals keep him apart from the other figures in Miles' world. Book text, PDF 130.

### Colour and light
- **Green means evil, discomfort and apprehension across the whole film.** Doc Ock's lab gets a green tint to warn the audience that bad things are about to happen. Thompson, PDF 107: *"There's also a green tint to the space because we chose green to be the color of evil, discomfort, and apprehension in our film. It's our way of cuing the audience that bad things are about to happen."* The final Doc Ock paint (PDF 115) is lit chartreuse against a black-to-olive gradient.
- **The Hudson Valley forest is defined by the orange and red of fall leaves.** Book text, PDF 116.
- **Peni's colour concept (Kassai, PDF 134):** "She's two-toned." Her 30th-century school uniform has a shirt with a "technological sheen", a sweater "laced with reflective components", and "luminescent accents throughout."

### Environment design

#### Doc Ock's lab (Alchemax), PDF 106–114
- **Cold and sanitized.** Thompson, PDF 107. Believable rather than super high-tech: *"A lot of times in science fiction movies, there is a tendency to go super high-tech, but we wanted to be believable as well. You have to believe that this is a place where scientists can actually do their work."*
- **Reflective surfaces form a "subtle hall of mirrors"** as Spider-Man and Miles walk through the lab. Thompson, PDF 107.
- **Real experimental tech is unfinished-looking, not slick consumer plastic.** Thompson, PDF 114: *"When you look at real experimental, cutting edge science you'll see that most of the early prototypes aren't actually that slick. The technology isn't covered up with slick plastic and doesn't look like an iPod. We wanted Doc Ock's world to feel cold, sanitized, utilitarian, and experimental, which gives everything a heightened sense of eeriness for an animated movie."*
- **Robh Ruppel developed the lab's style through grayscale value studies.** Caption, PDF 110. O'Keefe painted the lab (PDF 106–109, 112–113).
- **The high-tech lab sits "nestled in the hills of the Hudson Valley".** It is also where Spider-Gwen is first revealed. Thompson, PDF 116.

#### Hudson Valley forest, PDF 116–119
- **Why a forest:** audiences had already seen Peter swing from skyscrapers and jump on subway trains. Phil Lord and Chris Miller wanted "an organic way to get Spider-Man out of the city", so we see him swing in the forest. Thompson, PDF 116.
- **Trees were tripled in size but kept as graphic as possible.** O'Keefe, PDF 118.
- **Tree limbs are disconnected and the foliage floats,** after Bill Watterson's forest in *Calvin and Hobbes*. O'Keefe, PDF 118: *"Many of the tree limbs are disconnected, and most of the foliage is still floating. I was very inspired by Bill Watterson's forest from the Calvin and Hobbes strip. There is a clustering of shapes and details that describe the whole forest as a single vignette."*
- **Neil Ross and Mike Winkelmann painted concepts for "the Spider-Men's trip out of the city".** These are greener, foggy, coastal and near-photographic explorations. Caption, PDF 118.

#### Peter Parker's hideout (Spider Team Suit Up), PDF 126–129
- **Design question: what would a Spider-Man hideout look like if Peter had realized his full potential?** Yuhki Demers (artist), PDF 127: *"On the surface, it paints this Peter as an arrogant, sell-out egomaniac, but when you look deeper, we notice that this path has let him become a better, near-perfect Spider-Man and it will serve to inspire the rest of the team."*
- **References were modern architecture and the workplaces of successful people.** Demers, PDF 127: *"We tried to strike a balance between a place that's too pretty to work in and a place that actually functions … beneath all the sponsored swag, this Peter had dedicated his life to keeping the city safe."*
- **Props tell the backstory.** Caption, PDF 129: "A walk with the president, a wedding photo, mugshots, a hand-drawn thank you card, and multiple newspaper cut-outs" (Demers). Seen in the art:
  - branded Spidey soda cans (PDF 126) and a Spidey-O's cereal box
  - Daily Bugle front pages: "SPIDER-MAN SAVES NEW YORK AGAIN" and "GREEN GOBLIN?"
  - a "University of New York" diploma made out to Spider-Man, and a key to the city
  - a Spidey-mask guitar, vinyl record and clock, and an *Amazing Fantasy* comic
  - a Spider-Mobile dune buggy, a motorcycle, a gym with giant barbells, and a villain evidence board linked with red string
- **Ernie Rinard painted the hideout "filled with super hero gadgets".** Caption, PDF 129.

### FX
- **Collider / multiverse:** Peni and Noir are pulled into Miles' world by Prowler and Doc Ock's experiments with the Collider, "the Collider's ripping of the space-time continuum". Book text, PDF 134 and 138. PDF 107 shows the prototype collider ring in the lab: brass rings, a yellow ladder, and hazard-striped floor.
- **Glitch look (seen in the art, no text):** Ruppel's lab painting (PDF 111) smears objects into horizontal bars and offset rectangular blocks. See the visual section below.
- **Peni's energy (seen in the art):** Paul Lassaine's concept (PDF 136–137) wraps her in scribbled cyan light loops against a vortex of streaked orange and red paint cut by white-hot lightning.

### Character design

#### Doctor Octopus (Olivia Octavius), PDF 112–115
- **History:** she first appeared in *The Amazing Spider-Man* in July 1963, and Alfred Molina played her predecessor in *Spider-Man 2* (2004). This Doc Ock is "entirely new and original — a super intelligent and ambitious woman who is blinded by the possibilities of science." Book text, PDF 114.
- **She should look and feel like a regular scientist.** Lord and Miller wanted this from the start, and the team took a page from *Particle Fever*, the documentary about the physicists at the Large Hadron Collider. Shiyoon Kim (character designer), PDF 114: *"They wanted to convey the authenticity of these scientists who are so passionate about science that it can sometimes outweigh their ethics … They pushed us to look at real scientists to duplicate that same visual."*
- **Tentacles:** "synthetic semi-transparent silicon material that expands and contracts like membranes." Thompson and the vis-dev artists, PDF 115.
- **Costume: not slick or revealing, and it looks unfinished.** Thompson, PDF 115: *"we wanted the audience to be genuinely disturbed by how unrefined she seems. Her costume feels like something that hasn't been finished."*
- **Personality in the design:** she is "overly excited about the Multiverse and isn't really aware of how crazy she looks", with "wild hair with purple highlights". Thompson, PDF 115.
- **The tentacles are meant to disgust.** Thompson, PDF 115: *"I really wanted her organic, slimy tentacles to freak the audience out when they slide across Spider-Man's face. She's creepy and intimidating and she stands in high contrast with Spider-Man who is just a guy in tights."*
- **Credits:**
  - story art: Eva Bruschi (PDF 114)
  - concept costumes: Jesús Alonso Iglesias (PDF 114)
  - final design: 2D by Shiyoon Kim, 3D by Omar Smith, paint by Wendell Dalit (PDF 115)

#### Spider-Gwen, PDF 116, 120–125
- **Personality:** "Bright, extremely athletic and at the top of her game … seen by many as the most reliable and capable of all the Spider-characters." Book text, PDF 120.
- **Martial artist meets ballet dancer; the pointe shoes carry the idea.** Kristine Belson (President, Sony Pictures Animation), PDF 120: *"Nobody is cooler than Spider-Gwen … She is a tough fighter, but she also has this costume that makes her look genuinely feminine, which culminates in these elegant pointe shoes. It's like martial artist meets ballet dancer style … Her costume reflects that duality — this great mix of elegance and forceful spirit."*
- **Body: a tall, muscular dancer, not a "princess" body.** Shiyoon Kim, PDF 122: *"She is not just a pretty blonde girl with your typical thin princess body. We wanted her to look like a ballet dancer — tall, muscular, and powerful."*
- **Pipeline:** Kim did the initial sketches, Andrea Blasich made a sculpt from them, and vis-dev artist Omar Smith created the final design. Kim, PDF 122.
- **Credits:**
  - early concept: Kim (PDF 120)
  - suit turnaround: 2D by Kim, 3D by Omar Smith, paint by Naveen Selvanathan (PDF 121)
  - action poses: Kim and Justin K. Thompson (PDF 121)
  - concept design: Joey Chou (PDF 122)
  - facial expressions: Kim (PDF 122) and Borja Montoro (PDF 125)
  - painting: Alberto Mielgo (PDF 123)
  - sketches: Borja Montoro (PDF 124)
  - civilian final: 2D by Kim, 3D by Smith, paint by Wendell Dalit (PDF 124)
  - head paint: Naveen Selvanathan (PDF 124)
  - civilian sketches: Kim (PDF 125)

#### Spider-Ham (Peter Porker), PDF 130–133
- **Origin:** created by Tom DeFalco and Mark Armstrong in 1983 as "an homage to classic Looney Tunes characters." Book text, PDF 130.
- **Animation style:** rubber-hose, "visuals that are loose and graphic and stand out from the other figures in Miles' universe." Book text, PDF 130.
- **He follows the rules of his own animated universe** (the portable-hole gag). Book text, PDF 130.
- **Credits:**
  - final design: 2D by Craig Kellman, 3D by Omar Smith, paint by Justin K. Thompson (PDF 130)
  - sketches: Craig Kellman (PDF 131), Mark Ackland (PDF 132), Jim Mahfood (PDF 133)

#### Peni Parker and SP//dr, PDF 134–137
- **Premise:** Peni is a 14-year-old pilot of a mech suit called SP//dr. The suit's head houses a radioactive spider that shares a psychic link with the pilot. From Marvel's *Edge of Spider-Verse* miniseries. Book text, PDF 134.
- **SP//dr is "both familiar and of the future".** It keeps the original's humanoid form and adds two new technologies. Yashar Kassai, PDF 134: *"He sports two new technologies: a holographic display and a magnetic levitation."*
  - The holographic face display shows emotion: *"The display allows SP//dr to show an infinite number of faces to convey emotions. It was important for us to be able to convey his feelings to the audience visually."*
  - Magnetic levitation is why the limbs float as separate segments in the design.
- **Personality:** *"SP//dr is an incredible weapon, but for Peni, he is an even better friend. Beneath his anti-ballistic alloy shell, SP//dr can also be a big softy."* Kassai, PDF 134.
- **Peni design:** a "feisty Japanese girl from an anime universe" in a two-toned modern-day school uniform made of 30th-century fabrics (sheen, reflective components, luminescent accents). "As for the backpack … well, it's just very cute." Kassai, PDF 134.
- **Credits:**
  - final design sketches: Jesús Alonso Iglesias (PDF 134)
  - SP//dr concepts: Yashar Kassai and Tony Siruno; story art by Paul Watling (PDF 135)
  - SP//dr final: 2D design and paint by Kassai (PDF 135)
  - Peni final: 3D by Omar Smith, paint by Kassai (PDF 135, 137)
  - energy concept: Paul Lassaine (PDF 136–137)
  - cockpit artwork: Kassai (PDF 137)
  - early facial expressions: Shiyoon Kim (PDF 137)

#### Spider-Man Noir, PDF 138–140
- **Origin:** created by David Hine, Fabrice Sapolsky and Carmine Di Giandomenico, first appearing in 2009. "Modeled after an archetypal 1930s gumshoe, he offers a darker take on the all-American, optimistic Peter Parker character." Book text, PDF 138.
- **Animation frees the depiction of "this gun-wielding version of Spider-Man".** He is rendered in gritty black-and-white with cross-hatching. Thompson, PDF 140.
- **Credits:**
  - silhouette painting ("The mysterious Spider-Man Noir"): Yashar Kassai (PDF 138)
  - action concepts: Jesús Alonso Iglesias (PDF 139, and likely the PDF 140 panel)

### Story and production
- **The collider sequence is seen from the sidelines.** Chris Miller (Executive Producer), PDF 110: *"At some point, you are watching this classic Spider-Man movie through the eyes of one of the characters standing on the sidelines. It's great to have this fresh perspective of someone looking at the sequence from a hundred yards away."* This is the film's framing of a classic-Spidey fight seen through Miles.
- **Gwen's first reveal is in the forest near the lab.** Thompson, PDF 116. Dalit's story frames (PDF 116) show the beat: Peter and Miles swing through the canopy, Gwen stands hands on hips, the pair get caught in a web, and each pulls off his mask.
- **The team felt unusually free.** Lord, PDF 130: *"That level of fun and freedom gives us a lot of joy!"*
- **Omar Smith did the 3D design** of Doc Ock, Gwen (suit and civilian), Spider-Ham and Peni. The 2D-design → 3D-design → paint-over chain is the standard final-design pipeline in this section.

**PDF 108–109 (spread, O'Keefe): the lab at night in fog.**
- Palette:
  - slate navy #2e343e, mid slate #4a5560, fog #8a949a → #b8c0bc
  - windows flat chartreuse-yellow #d8d040 / #e8c030
  - olive-brown autumn canopy #6a5a30
  - pale birch trunks #c8ccc8
- **Lit windows are flat yellow rectangles.** Interior equipment inside them is simplified into lighter/darker yellow shapes (#c0b840 / #a8a040) with thin cream lines. There is no bloom and no gradient.
- **The glass facade reflects trees as darker speckled silhouettes.** A salt/spatter texture of pale specks sits on the dark slate.
- **Depth comes from stacked fog layers.** Each tree layer further back is lighter and lower in contrast; the farthest trees are barely darker than the fog.
- **Particles are tiny white squares and diamonds (about 3–6 px), not circles.** They are scattered sparsely like snow or spores.
- **Composition:**
  - The building is a sharp diagonal wedge thrusting in from the left, with horizontal mullion lines in near-black.
  - A tiny Spider-Man silhouette leaps off the cantilever tip into huge negative space (right half of 109).
  - A flock of birds is drawn as small arrowhead shapes.

**PDF 110–111 (spread). Robh Ruppel: Doc Ock's lab style studies and painting.**
- *Three grayscale thumbnails (PDF 110):*
  - wide, symmetrical one-point perspective with glossy grid floors reflecting the ceiling
  - a dark ceiling with a semicircular cut-out
  - white egg chairs in rows
  - They are pure value studies, built from 4–5 values.
- *Spread painting:*
  - **The foreground is a near-black silhouette** (drafting table, dark wall) occupying the left third, framing a lit midground.
  - On the dark plum wall (#2a1526 / #3f2733) is a giant **dripping tentacle-blob emblem with concentric ring lines** in muted mauve (#6a3050, rings #8a3a5a). There is also a stone pillar with a crackle texture (#5a4a58).
  - The midground is a warm wood-slat ceiling (#7a4a30 / #a06840) over 1970s reel-to-reel mainframes in purple (#4a2a5a) with hot-pink top strips (#e06a9a).
  - A hanging orrery sculpture: flat yellow spheres (#f2e020), olive rings (#8a8a30), a glossy red dish (#b02838).
  - A glossy grey floor (#9aa0b0) shows vertical reflection streaks, and there is an orange rug with swirl pattern (#d8502a / #f08050).
  - A glass wall opens to a cold, pale blue-white exterior (#a8c8e0 / #e0f0ff).
- **Glitch smear (zoomed):**
  - The orrery and dish are sliced into **horizontal bars stretched sideways.** Some slices are shifted left or right as rectangular blocks, and pixels are dragged into long streaks of the object's own colour (red dish → long red bars, yellow sphere → blocky yellow rectangles).
  - No RGB split here: it is displacement and stretching, a "datamosh" look.

## Recipes

1. **Villain-zone colour cue.** Whenever a scene belongs to Doc Ock or the collider, multiply the whole frame toward sage-green (#9aa878). Give lights a chartreuse tint (#c8e83a). Keep surfaces white or grey and highly reflective: mirror the floor with a vertically flipped, 30–40%-opacity copy. Add ceiling light fixtures as **flat white octagon or hexagon outline rings** (stroke only, no glow).

2. **Halftone glow instead of bloom.** Around an emissive point, draw a dot grid (pitch 6–8 px, rotated 45°) inside a radius R. Set each dot's radius to `maxR * (1 - d/R)^1.5` in the light colour. Draw the light itself as a flat bright disc. Do not blur. (Doc Ock belt lights, PDF 115.)

3. **Halftone contact shadow.** Under a character, draw an ellipse (width about 1.3× the stance, height about 0.15× the width). Fill it with a 45° dot grid in #4a4a4a whose dot radius falls off from the centre to zero at the rim. No soft shadow. For Noir, use **horizontal hatch lines** in the same ellipse instead: line spacing about 1% of figure height, each line clipped to the ellipse, stroke width tapering toward the ends. (PDF 130, 138.)

4. **Hard rim band with misregistration.**
   - Take the silhouette mask, offset it 3–6 px away from the key light, and subtract it to get a crescent band.
   - Fill the band with a light colour (#b890e0 for Gwen, #c040b0 magenta for Miles), overlaid with white halftone dots.
   - Stroke a 1–2 px darker offset line (#7a4ad0) just outside it on the shadow side.
   - Leave the silhouette edge itself dissolving into a 2–3 px dot fringe. (PDF 121, 112, 130.)

5. **Shade coloured costume panels with hatching, not gradients.** On the shadow side of red panels, draw parallel lines in the panel colour (#c02030) over black, aligned with the limb's long axis and 3–4 px apart. For black fabric, put a dark-tinted dot screen (#3a0a2a on #10050a) in the half-lit zone. (Miles, PDF 112.)

6. **Halftone as fabric texture.** Give black or navy suits a fixed-screen dot or hex-mesh pattern one step lighter than the base (Gwen: #2a2830 on #0c0a10; Peni's vest: #4a5a90 on #1e2440). Scale dot size with the lit amount (N·L) so the pattern also does the shading.

7. **Autumn forest (O'Keefe), worm's-eye view.**
   - Background: a vertical gradient from cream (#fbe6a8) at the top-centre sun to orange (#f0b040).
   - Trunks: long tapered quads converging to a vanishing point above the frame, in maroon (#5a1c1c) with a lighter side stripe (#8a3a2a) and speckle noise.
   - Foliage: clusters of 5–15 flat overlapping blobs per tree in #e07830, #c83a28 and #f0b040, **floating without connecting branches**. Scale the trees 3× what feels natural.
   - Depth: fade far trunks toward cream haze, not blue. Characters are small, dark silhouettes.

8. **Foggy night exterior.**
   - Stack 4–6 flat tree-silhouette layers from #2e343e (near) to #b8c0bc (far).
   - Buildings are sharp diagonal wedges with near-black mullion lines. Windows are flat #d8d040 rectangles with interior objects as lighter and darker yellow flat shapes.
   - Reflect tree silhouettes on the glass at lower opacity with a spatter texture.
   - Sprinkle white 3–6 px **square** particles sparsely. Birds are tiny arrowheads.

9. **Glitch smear (collider interference).**
   - Pick random horizontal bands, 8–40 px tall.
   - For each band, either offset it horizontally by ±20–120 px as a hard-edged block, or stretch its leftmost or rightmost column into a long bar of that colour.
   - Keep the object's own colours; no RGB split is needed for this variant. Animate on twos with new random bands each step. (Ruppel, PDF 111.)

10. **Speed background (Joey Chou).** Behind a running character, fill the frame with horizontal dry-brush streaks: random-length rounded rectangles with ragged ends in #f0a0d8, #e060b0 and white. Draw the character as a **flat white fill with one 3–4 px hot-pink contour (#e0307a)** and no internal shading. (PDF 122.)

11. **Hero-hideout staging.**
    - Black void (#050308) with 20–40 thin (1 px) straight web strands in #c0c0c8 criss-crossing as architecture.
    - Glossy red floor (#c0204a → #e03060) with black stripe bands and vertical reflection streaks.
    - Floating translucent blue panels (#5080e0 at 50% opacity, 1 px lighter border) arranged in an arc.
    - Floating chamfered platforms. Use hard red/blue split lighting for interiors (key #e02030, fill #2a3a90).

12. **SP//dr construction.**
    - An egg-shaped body in red/blue panel splits (#b01e2e / #2e3aa8).
    - A black visor with emissive red angular eye shapes that swap per emotion (the "infinite faces" display).
    - Limbs are separate floating segments with 10–20 px gaps and no joints, bobbing independently on sine offsets (magnetic levitation), with a faint violet glow in the gaps.
    - An orange radial halo on top of the head. Add sticker decals and scratch lines.

13. **Per-character render rules in one frame.** Keep a style flag per character and never unify them:
    - *Spider-Ham:* 2 heads tall, rubber-hose, tapered thick black web lines, cartoon FX (circling stars, radial impact ticks).
    - *Peni:* about 5 heads tall, flat cel with near-zero shading, black hair as a single spiky silhouette, anime symbols (anger vein, sweat drop, sparkle eyes).
    - *Noir:* black silhouette plus grey edge slivers and hatch shadows only, no colour at all.
    - *Gwen:* about 8.5 heads, en-pointe feet, white/black/magenta.

14. **Noir three-value action frames.** Use only #000, one flat grey (#9a9898) laid as broad marker strokes that ignore form, and white paper. Draw buildings as clusters of vertical black bars, put a big white moon disc behind the silhouette, and use strong dutch angles. (PDF 139.)

15. **Mielgo-style portrait shading.** Build faces from angular flat planes. Colour skin shadows violet or mauve (#b07890) rather than a darker skin tone. Lay patches of fine parallel hatching (1 px lines, 2–3 px apart) over hair and shadow planes, and scatter a few loose unfilled structural strokes on top. (PDF 123.)

## Best quotes

4. "We have included a lot of line work in the characters' performances. We wanted to break up forms in a way that audiences can't quite tell whether something is 3D or not." — Josh Beveridge, Animation Supervisor (PDF 130)

6. "One of design's greatest pleasures is stark contrast." — Yashar Kassai, artist (PDF 134)
7. "We depict him in gritty black-and-white, because that's the world he is coming from. There's a lot of cross-hatching involved with Noir." — Justin K. Thompson (PDF 140)
